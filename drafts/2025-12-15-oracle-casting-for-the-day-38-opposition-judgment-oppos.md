# Opposition and Reflections: Family, Health, and the Fog of War

I was sipping my cup of cacao this morning, staring at the Oracle reading on my screen—38, Opposition. The judgment hit close: “In small matters, good fortune.” It got me thinking about the push and pull in my life right now, especially after my weekly call with my parents. There’s always this undercurrent of opposition—geographic, emotional, even philosophical—that seems to thread through our conversations. Let me unpack it a bit.

My dad, as usual, circled back to the hope of a reunion between me, my daughter Mulan, and her mom, Sonia. I can feel his longing through the phone, but I don’t want him fixating on an outcome that, structurally, looks improbable. Geographically, we’re at opposite ends—me setting up Agroverse.shop here, them rooted elsewhere. It’s like plotting points on a map that just don’t connect. I wish I could ease his mind, but I’m not sure how to without giving false hope.

Then there’s my mom, who finally opened up about the loss of two of her sisters this year to diabetes. She also mentioned a cousin dealing with the same illness. It’s heavy, hearing her process that grief—her third younger sister and her youngest, gone. Health feels like this silent opposition looming over us all. She’s worried about me too, keeps nudging me to get a full body checkup since it’s been a few years. “You’re not getting younger, Gary,” she says, and I can hear the concern about the financial risks I’ve taken on with my ventures. Retirement, she hints. Sonia brought it up too during a FaceTime with Mulan, asking about Singapore’s system. I had to bite my tongue a bit—honestly, with the inverse pyramid of demographics, paying into a pension feels like betting on a Ponzi scheme. Mathematically, it doesn’t add up. Future generations won’t outnumber us to sustain the payouts. It’s a modern construct anyway, this idea of “retirement.” Pre-industrial times? No such thing. People worked till they dropped—often not living long enough to even think about stopping.

That got me down a rabbit hole about late-stage life. I was chatting with Tiffin out at Harbin Hot Springs recently, musing on how perceptions of aging and death have shifted. Ancient Greek philosophers like Socrates—he willingly drank hemlock for his principles. Oda Nobunaga in Japan chose seppuku over capture. Seneca and Epicurus both argued that if life’s value tips negative, why cling to it? Contrast that with today’s healthcare obsession: keep alive at all costs. I think of my eldest aunt on my dad’s side, bedridden for a decade post-stroke, just… existing. My grandmother, too. What kind of life is that? If I’m honest, I lean toward euthanasia when the calculus doesn’t make sense anymore. Opposition again—modern systems versus personal philosophy.

Then there’s family dynamics, another layer of opposition. My mom brought up a cousin from my dad’s side—the one who borrowed a hefty sum from my friend Jerry here in the US. No updates on repayment, just crickets when I poke him. My mom’s critical, Sonia apparently had a bad vibe about him when they met (news to me), and even Fatima flagged him before the loan happened. Seems the women around me have sharper radar for red flags than I do. Jerry’s gracious about it, but his wife? Not so much. Chuckles. I told this cousin last time we spoke, “I’m not connected to high net worth folks anymore, no point reaching out.” I’ve set my LinkedIn and social media to avoid these messy entanglements. For a while, the only messages I got were pitches for money—strange dynamic, man. Fatima was spot-on advising me to stop entertaining such requests. “You’re too helpful, Gary, and it bites you in credibility,” she said. Hindsight? She’s right. Again.

This cousin drama ties into deeper family tensions, I think—my mom’s side (Teochew) versus my dad’s (Hokkien). It’s mellowed over decades, but the friction’s still there. Is it cultural, ethnic, or just clashing values? I wonder. Even the startup world’s “friends, family, and fools” fundraising model—calling them “angels”—feels like a headache when you see these undercurrents. This loan wasn’t even my project; I just helped because he reached out in need. Facepalm.

Key observation: opposition isn’t just external—it’s internal too. I’m wrestling with whether my current path, growing Agroverse.shop passively (setting the frame, waiting for conversions rather than aggressive sales), is the best use of my limited time. Same with the Truesight DAO community. Is this where my energy should go? Hindsight’s 20/20, foresight’s a fog of war. Right now, the path pulls me here until a stronger signal emerges.

Reflections for the day: Life’s full of these opposing forces—family expectations versus reality, health versus risk, modern systems versus ancient wisdom. I’m not sure I have answers, just a lot of questions. What about you—how do you navigate the oppositions in your life? Where do you draw the line between pushing forward and letting go? I’d love to hear your thoughts while I sip another cup of cacao and mull over mine.

# Opposition and Wealth: Family Expectations, Financial Struggles, and Badges of Honor

I’m sitting here with my cup of cacao, still digesting yesterday’s Oracle reading—38, Opposition—and the latest chat with my mom. It’s funny how a single conversation can peel back so many layers of tension, especially around money and family expectations. There’s always this push and pull, like I’m caught between what’s expected and what’s real. Let me break it down.

During the call, it clicked why Sonia hasn’t been pressing me on the monthly child support I’d agreed to. I’ve had to dial back due to the financial load this year—Trump’s turbulence didn’t help, shaking up markets and my own stability. Reading between the lines from a convo with Sonia a few weeks ago, and now with my mom filling in gaps, it seems my parents have quietly wired her cash for Mulan’s expenses. Again. And honestly, between that, support from Sonia’s parents, and her own salary, it sounds like there’s more than enough. Maybe even a surplus she’s speculating with—god knows on what. I told her to ease up, focus on Mulan, and didn’t dive into investment advice. I’m not in a place to play financial guru, and frankly, I don’t want to.

That ties into a quip from Garis during a FaceTime call a while back. He jokingly called me a 败家子—basically, a squanderer of ancestral wealth. Chuckles. Good at cutting expenses, terrible at generating revenue or growing it through my ventures. It stung a bit, but I’ve heard this kind of thing before. It’s such a Chinese cultural lens—everything centered on wealth and prosperity. If you’re not building the family fortune, you’re tearing it down. These negative labels, though? I’m used to them by now. I just set them aside. Humorously, I think of them as badges of honor—each critique a little pin on my chest for surviving another round of judgment.

Key observation: Opposition here isn’t just about money—it’s about values. My parents, and even broader family, see financial success as a duty, a legacy. Me? I’m wrestling with ventures like Agroverse.shop, taking risks that don’t always pay off (yet), and prioritizing different things—like time with Mulan when I can, or building something meaningful over chasing quick cash. And Sonia’s situation, with this apparent surplus, adds another layer. I’m glad Mulan’s taken care of, but I can’t help wondering if I’m being judged for not contributing more directly right now, even if circumstances are out of my hands.

Reflections for the day: This cultural weight around wealth feels like a constant tug-of-war. I get the importance of stability—hell, I feel the pressure every day—but I’m not sure I buy into the idea that it’s the only measure of worth. I’ve taken hits, sure, but I’m still here, still trying. Maybe that’s the real badge of honor. What about you—how do you handle family expectations around money? Do you feel that cultural pull, or have you carved your own metric for “success”? I’m curious to hear while I sip my cacao and figure out my next move in this fog of opposition.
