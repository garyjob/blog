I’m sitting here with a warm cup of cacao, staring at the oracle readings from this morning, and something’s clicking in a way it hasn’t before. The cards—18, “Work on What Has Been Spoiled,” and 57, “The Gentle,” paired with today’s draw of 28, “Preponderance of the Great,” and 48, “The Well”—they’re nudging me toward a realization I’ve been dodging for a while. It’s about how my ingrained habits of discipline and consistency, the very traits I’ve been taught to wear like a badge of honor, might actually be tripping me up in spaces like DAOs and collaborative projects. Let’s unpack this, because I think there’s a pattern here worth wrestling with.

### Title: “Doing Less, Seeing More: Why My ‘Founder’ Label Is a Misstep”

#### The Weight of Being the “Founder” (When I’m Not)
I’ve been reflecting on how I keep ending up as the so-called “founder” of projects—whether it’s guilds, startups, or tech initiatives. Truth is, I never started any of them. The real catalysts, the ones who sparked these ideas, often ghosted or faded out early on. New folks come in, see me holding the fort, and assume I’m the origin story. It’s flattering, sure, but it’s also a trap. I end up carrying the weight of something I didn’t even build from scratch—patching up what’s been spoiled, as the oracle puts it.

This pattern isn’t new. I saw it at my last gig, where I inherited 7 or 8 tech stacks because the original engineering managers either chased the next shiny thing or jumped ship for a fatter paycheck. I was left juggling a mess of codebases, managing four junior engineers who—honestly—were less productive than the LLMs we’ve got today. It was exhausting, and I couldn’t help but wonder: why am I always the one cleaning up?

#### Discipline as a Double-Edged Sword
Growing up, discipline and consistency were drilled into me—family values, startup culture, the whole tech grind. You show up, you execute, you deliver. But in the context of a DAO or any decentralized, collaborative space, that impulse to “get it done” can backfire. The oracle’s “The Gentle” card—success through what is small—feels like a quiet reminder to ease up. I’ve been too focused on pushing forward, on executing with precision, when maybe the real work is in stepping back and reading the room. Or, as I’m starting to see it, deciphering the collective will before deploying any resources.

There’s a prisoner’s dilemma at play here, isn’t there? If I keep doing too much, too fast, I’m not just burning myself out—I’m skewing the dynamic. Others don’t step up because they don’t have to. I’ve been conditioned to see inaction as failure, but what if inaction is the move? What if “doing nothing” is actually the most productive thing I can do while I figure out how to frame the problem? Einstein had it right—spend 55 minutes thinking about the problem, 5 on the solution. Scott Fitzgerald’s take hits even harder: hold two opposing ideas in your head at once—wasting time twiddling thumbs is unproductive, yet gazing into the abyss to spot the real pattern is everything—and still keep moving.

#### Barely Enough: A New Way to Collaborate
A piece of advice from a friend, Johnson, keeps echoing in my head. When dealing with business partners, don’t build the whole damn solution. Do just enough—barely enough—to leave a gap for the other party to fill with their own resources. If they step up, you add a bit more. If they don’t, you’ve saved yourself the hassle of over-investing. The worst-case scenario? You become the accidental CEO of a project you never meant to own. I’ve been there, and I’m starting to see how I’ve been played by this dynamic in the past. Even my dad saw it years ago with a business partner in Singapore—warned me about being exploited for my “do everything” streak, a trait my family cultivated as a virtue.

This feels especially critical now as we bring in more partners outside the voting rights pool in our current network. More players mean more risk, more capital needed to keep things liquid. If I keep overextending, I’m not just risking burnout—I’m risking the whole structure. The oracle’s “The Well” reading today feels like a grounding force: the town may change, but the well stays constant. Maybe it’s telling me to anchor myself in what’s essential, not what’s urgent.

#### Reflections for the Day: Guarding the Gap
So here’s where I’m landing—guarding against my own impulse to over-deliver starts with small, intentional shifts. I’m taking a cue from another bit of advice I got recently: don’t do a full brain dump when communicating. Share just enough. Let others pull for more if they want it. It’s not about withholding—it’s about balance. It’s about leaving space for the collective to show up.

Key observation: “Work on What Has Been Spoiled” doesn’t mean fix everything yourself. It means recognize what’s broken and approach it gently, with small steps, as “The Gentle” suggests. I’m starting to think that my role isn’t to be the founder or the fixer—it’s to be the one who holds space for the right questions, the right problems to surface.

What about you—do you find yourself overextending in collaborative spaces, or are you better at leaving gaps for others to fill? How do you balance the urge to act with the need to wait for clarity? I’m curious, because I’m still figuring this out myself, one oracle draw and cup of cacao at a time.
