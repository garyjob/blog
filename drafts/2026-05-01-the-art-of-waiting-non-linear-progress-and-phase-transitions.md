I was sitting across from Denis the other day, over a hearty lunch at his new restaurant space, listening to him unpack the wild ride of the past year. He’d been through the wringer—booted out of his old spot in downtown Menlo Park after 25 years, bleeding cash in a ghost kitchen, teetering on the edge of shutting down. Then, out of nowhere, the landlord from the building just across the parking lot reached out with an offer for a bigger, better space with more foot traffic. Just like that, a non-linear twist turned everything around. We talked for three hours, and as I sipped my cup of cacao, he said something that stuck with me: life’s often like this. You can’t force the big shifts. Striving too hard usually just burns you out.

That conversation got me reflecting on how the stuff that really matters—the breakthroughs, the serendipitous wins—rarely follows a straight line. It’s tempting to measure success by tasks ticked off or hours billed, but those are the things that are easy to automate. The real progress, the kind that feels like a lightning strike, resists being boxed into a spreadsheet. It’s messy, unpredictable, and often shows up when you least expect it. Like when I casually mentioned a bottleneck I’ve been wrestling with—processing cacao nibs into chocolate bars—and Denis, offhand, offered up his kitchen as a solution. I hadn’t even thought to ask. I’d just come from dropping off cacao at Will’s workplace on the Stanford organic farm, kicking myself for forgetting to bring it up there, and then—bam—here was a fix, over a casual chat.

## Phase Transitions: Chaos Theory and Life’s Unpredictable Shifts

This whole idea of non-linear progress reminds me of something I dove into years back: chaos theory and the concept of phase transitions. If you’ve never come across it, a phase transition is that moment when a system flips from one state to another—like water turning to steam at a boiling point. You can’t predict exactly when or how it’ll happen, but you can set up the conditions and wait. Life feels a lot like that sometimes. You can’t automate or force these shifts; you can only prepare the field and stay alert.

I’ve been thinking about historical examples of this, too. Take Zhuge Liang, the legendary strategist from the Three Kingdoms era in ancient China. This guy was a master of playing the long game, waiting for the right phase transition to tip things his way. There’s a story from the Battle of Red Cliff where General Zhou Yu gave him an impossible task: produce 100,000 arrows in three weeks or face execution. Zhuge didn’t panic or grind himself into the ground. He chilled for over two weeks, then, on a foggy night, sent ships loaded with straw figures to the enemy’s side, provoking them to fire arrows all night long. By dawn, he’d collected way more than he needed. No sweat.

Then there’s the second moment at Red Cliff, where he somehow convinced the enemy general to chain all his warships together to prevent seasickness—a brilliant idea, right? Except when the wind shifted (as Zhuge likely anticipated), his side fired flaming arrows, turning the linked fleet into a giant bonfire. The enemy retreated, and the campaign was over. Reading between the lines, it’s clear Zhuge relied on a metaphysical framework, something akin to the I-Ching or Book of Changes, to read the potential states of nature and human behavior. He’d set up the dominos, conserve his energy, and wait for the right moment to nudge things in his favor.

## Setting the Field in Modern Times

Key observation: I think there’s something to this “set the field and wait” approach, even today. Part of why I’ve been experimenting with a minimalist lifestyle—living out of my car, relying on food banks—is to reduce my own caloric load on the system. It gives me breathing room to observe, to sense the patterns in the field, to wait for those phase transitions without burning out. And honestly, I’m testing whether this ancient framework, this Dao (as in Taoist philosophy), can apply to modern life—or if it’s just mystical nonsense.

Here’s what that looks like in practice. I’ve been mulling over how AI fits into this. If used right, AI can strip away the routine busywork—the desk-bound grind that keeps us from stepping into the field. A conversation I had recently at a Stanford research center reinforced this: AI can free up time, but only if we resist falling into Jevons Paradox, where efficiency just creates more work. (Side note—my daily draws from an AI-augmented oracle have been hammering this point home: don’t let deskwork breed more deskwork.) Instead, that freed-up time should go toward physically sensing what’s happening in the larger environment, tuning how we set up our dominos while we wait for the theoretical shifts.

## The Human Edge: Field Sense in an Automated World

Until we crack Moravec’s and Polanyi’s paradoxes—those stubborn truths about how humans intuitively grasp things machines can’t, like navigating a messy real-world environment—field sense remains our biggest asset. It’s not about cranking out more tasks; it’s about being present, noticing the subtle cues, and positioning yourself for the non-linear leaps when they come. Denis didn’t force his new restaurant space; he endured the grind until the right opportunity emerged. Zhuge Liang didn’t build 100,000 arrows; he waited for the fog and the enemy’s paranoia to do the work.

Reflections for the evening: I’m left wondering how much of our modern hustle is just pointless striving. What if the real skill is learning to wait, to conserve energy, to read the field? And with AI taking over the predictable stuff, shouldn’t we be doubling down on the human knack for sensing what’s next? What about you—how do you balance the grind with the art of waiting for life’s phase transitions?
